\section{Cooling infrastructure as a first-class actuarial risk}\label{sec:cooling}

The actuarial literature on data centers has traditionally treated
cooling as a derivative of power: heat is a by-product of IT load, and
cooling failures are folded into a general ``machinery breakdown''
category. This is empirically and economically inadequate. As of
2025, cooling failures account for 12\% of public outages
\citep{Uptime2024}, but the severity distribution of cooling-induced
events has shifted dramatically with the adoption of AI training
workloads, lithium-ion battery rooms, and liquid/immersion cooling
architectures. This section elevates cooling to a first-class peril
with its own hazard rate, severity tail, and premium-loading
implications, and ties every cooling-engineering quantity back to the
actuarial state variables $(\lambda, \xi, \sigma, U, P^{\mathrm{tech}})$
of the main framework.

\subsection{Why cooling deserves its own actuarial treatment}\label{ss:cool-why}

Three structural shifts have made cooling an independent
underwriting concern rather than a sub-peril of property damage:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
   \item \textbf{Density.} Rack power densities have risen from
         $\approx\SI{10}{\kilo\watt\per rack}$ in 2015 to
         $\SI{30}{}$--$\SI{120}{\kilo\watt\per rack}$ in 2025 for
         AI-training racks. Air cooling alone is thermodynamically
         insufficient above $\sim\SI{30}{\kilo\watt\per rack}$.
   \item \textbf{Coupling.} Liquid and immersion cooling place fluids
         in direct contact with energised electrical equipment.
         A leak ceases to be a property damage event and becomes a
         power-electrical coupled event with cascading SLA and
         business-interruption consequences.
   \item \textbf{Climate exposure.} Cooling efficiency depends on
         outdoor wet-bulb temperature. The hazard rate is therefore
         a non-stationary function of climate, with seasonality and
         long-run drift that classical property catastrophe modelling
         does not capture.
\end{enumerate}

We will see that each of these maps cleanly to a distinct
modification of the hazard rate $\lambda_t$
of~\eqref{eq:hazard}, the GPD tail shape $\xi$ of~\eqref{eq:POT}, and
the premium loading $\theta$ of~\eqref{eq:final}.

\subsection{The thermodynamic engine: physics that drives the hazard}\label{ss:cool-thermo}

The cooling system is a heat engine operating in reverse, characterised
by its coefficient of performance (COP):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:COP}
   \mathrm{COP} \;=\; \frac{\dot Q_{\mathrm{cool}}}{\dot W_{\mathrm{in}}},
\end{equation}
where $\dot Q_{\mathrm{cool}}$ is the rate of heat removal from the IT
load and $\dot W_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the electrical power consumed by the
cooling plant itself. For a Carnot-limited chiller operating between
cold-aisle temperature $T_c$ and outdoor wet-bulb $T_h$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Carnot}
   \mathrm{COP}_{\mathrm{Carnot}} \;=\;
   \frac{T_c}{T_h - T_c}.
\end{equation}
Real chillers achieve $30$--$60\%$ of the Carnot limit. The cooling
plant's electrical load is therefore
$\dot W_{\mathrm{in}}= \dot Q_{\mathrm{cool}}/\mathrm{COP}$, and the
data center's PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) decomposes as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PUE-decomp}
   \mathrm{PUE} \;=\; 1 \;+\; \frac{1}{\mathrm{COP}\,\eta_{\mathrm{UPS}}}
                          \;+\; \varepsilon_{\mathrm{lighting,etc}}.
\end{equation}
A critical consequence: as $T_h$ rises (climate change, heatwave),
$\mathrm{COP}$ falls hyperbolically, and the chiller draws more power
to deliver the same cooling capacity. Once the chiller saturates at
its rated motor capacity, additional load cannot be cooled and a
\emph{thermal envelope failure} ensues. This is the chain that
converts a weather event into an actuarial claim, and we encode it in
\eqref{eq:lambda-cool} below.

\paragraph{Wet-bulb temperature and the actuarial covariate.}
Cooling capacity is governed not by dry-bulb (air) temperature but by
\emph{wet-bulb} temperature $T_{\mathrm{wb}}$, the lowest temperature
achievable by adiabatic evaporation. The wet-bulb approximation
\citep{Stull2011} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wetbulb}
   T_{\mathrm{wb}} \;\approx\; T \arctan\!\bigl[0.151977(\mathrm{RH} + 8.313659)^{1/2}\bigr]
   + \arctan(T + \mathrm{RH}) - \arctan(\mathrm{RH}-1.676331)
   + 0.00391838\,\mathrm{RH}^{3/2}\arctan(0.023101\,\mathrm{RH}) - 4.686035,
\end{equation}
with $T$ in $^\circ$C and $\mathrm{RH}$ in \%. This is the
\emph{correct} climate covariate for the cooling hazard process.

\subsection{An Arrhenius-type cooling hazard}\label{ss:cool-hazard}

Define the cooling-stress factor
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cool-stress}
   \Psi(t) \;=\; \frac{\dot Q_{\mathrm{IT}}(t)}{\dot Q_{\mathrm{cool},\max}\,(1-U_{\mathrm{cool}})}.
\end{equation}
This is the ratio of instantaneous IT heat output to available cooling
capacity. $\Psi=1$ corresponds to a fully saturated cooling plant;
$\Psi>1$ is thermodynamic infeasibility. The cooling-induced
instantaneous failure hazard is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda-cool}
   \boxed{\;
   \lambda^{\mathrm{cool}}_t \;=\; \lambda^{\mathrm{cool}}_0
   \cdot \exp\!\bigl(\delta_T (T_{\mathrm{wb}}(t)-T^*_{\mathrm{wb}})\bigr)
   \cdot \Psi(t)^{\gamma_\Psi}
   \cdot \1\{\,T_{\mathrm{wb}}(t)>T^*_{\mathrm{wb}}\,\},\;}
\end{equation}
where $T^*_{\mathrm{wb}}$ is the chiller-design wet-bulb temperature,
$\delta_T\in[0.08,0.20]$ per kelvin is the wet-bulb stress
coefficient calibrated from chiller derating curves, and
$\gamma_\Psi \in [2.5,4]$ is a saturation exponent that captures the
sharp non-linearity as $\Psi\to 1$. The right-hand side
of~\eqref{eq:lambda-cool} is the cooling analogue of
the Arrhenius--voltage--cyber decomposition in the electrical hazard
\eqref{eq:hazard}. The composite hazard for the IT load is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda-total}
   \lambda_t^{\mathrm{IT}} \;=\;
   \underbrace{\lambda_t^{\mathrm{elec}}}_{\text{from }\eqref{eq:hazard}}
   \;+\;\lambda^{\mathrm{cool}}_t
   \;-\; \lambda_t^{\mathrm{elec}}\lambda^{\mathrm{cool}}_t \,\dd t
   \;\approx\; \lambda_t^{\mathrm{elec}} + \lambda^{\mathrm{cool}}_t,
\end{equation}
the last step holding for the small instantaneous rates of practical
interest.

liquid-immersion plants, the choice of working fluid drives the
catastrophic-loss tail, and an actuary cannot price the cover without
understanding the chemistry.

\paragraph{Phase-change kinetics.}
Two-phase immersion exploits the Clausius--Clapeyron relation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CC}
   \frac{\dd P}{\dd T} \;=\; \frac{L_{\mathrm{vap}}}{T\,\Delta v},
\end{equation}
where $L_{\mathrm{vap}}$ is the latent heat of vaporisation and
$\Delta v$ the specific-volume change on phase change. The
attraction of two-phase cooling is the enormous heat-removal capacity
at constant temperature: a phase change at \SI{60}{\celsius} can
remove $\sim\SI{1500}{\kilo\watt\per rack}$ at quasi-constant
$T$. The corresponding actuarial vulnerability is that any
perturbation that shifts the saturation curve (impurity ingress,
non-condensable gases, condenser-coil fouling) destroys the
isothermal advantage, and the system silently degrades until a
sudden over-temperature event.

\paragraph{PFAS regulatory risk.}
3M, the principal manufacturer of fluorinated coolants
(Novec, FC family), announced in 2022 a phase-out of PFAS chemistry
by the end of 2025. The replacement supply chain is still
consolidating in 2026. Underwriters offering full-replacement-value
property cover on immersion-cooled facilities must price
\emph{coolant-replacement risk} alongside hardware-replacement
risk: a regulatory ban can render a working installation
non-restorable to original specification. This creates an
\emph{insurance-recoverable PFAS contingent liability} that is
analogous in spirit to the asbestos cover crisis of the late
twentieth century. The Wang transform pricing of
\S\ref{ss:loadings} should be applied to this exposure with
$\lambda^*>0$ corresponding to a market-implied catastrophe load.

We partition the cooling severity into two components:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cool-sev}
   X^{\mathrm{cool}} \;=\;
   X^{\mathrm{attr}}_{\mathrm{cool}} \;+\; X^{\mathrm{cat}}_{\mathrm{cool}},
\end{equation}
with body distribution lognormal $\ln X^{\mathrm{attr}}_{\mathrm{cool}}
\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{cool}},\sigma_{\mathrm{cool}}^2)$
fitted from maintenance-claim experience, and catastrophic component
GPD with shape $\xi_{\mathrm{cool}}$ and scale $\sigma_{\mathrm{cool}}$.
Empirical experience to date suggests
$\xi_{\mathrm{cool}}\in [0.3, 0.7]$ for liquid-cooled facilities ---
\emph{noticeably heavier than the electrical-side
$\xi_{\mathrm{elec}}\in[0.2,0.6]$ of \S\ref{sec:severity}}. The
underwriting consequence is a steeper tail-loading
$\theta_2$ in~\eqref{eq:final} for sites with liquid or immersion
cooling architecture.

\subsection{Water consumption and the WUE risk}\label{ss:cool-wue}

Cooling consumes water at a rate quantified by the Water Usage
Effectiveness (WUE) ratio:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:WUE}
   \mathrm{WUE} \;=\; \frac{\text{annual on-site water}}{\text{annual IT energy}}
   \quad [\mathrm{L/kWh}].
\end{equation}
Industry-average WUE for evaporative-cooled hyperscale facilities is
\SI{1.8}{\liter\per\kilo\watt\hour}; best-in-class adiabatic plants
reach \SI{0.2}{\liter\per\kilo\watt\hour}; dry-cooled (air) sites
approach zero. For a \SI{200}{\mega\watt} hyperscale at WUE = 1.8,
annual water draw is \SI{3.15}{\giga\liter} --- comparable to a
small town's municipal demand.

The actuarial relevance is twofold. First, water-scarcity
restrictions (already in force in Arizona, parts of Spain, and
sections of South Africa) can compel an operator to switch from
evaporative to dry cooling at short notice; the
COP loss raises $\Psi$ and through~\eqref{eq:lambda-cool} raises
$\lambda^{\mathrm{cool}}$. Second, water-rights litigation creates a
new third-party liability exposure that the standard
$L^{(i)}_{\mathrm{LIAB}}$ component of~\eqref{eq:loss-decomp} must now
explicitly carry.

\paragraph{South African application.} Cape Town's \emph{Day Zero}
water crisis of 2018 and Johannesburg's recurrent drought stress
make WUE a binding constraint on any African hyperscale build.
For an actuary pricing a Johannesburg campus, the
$L^{(i)}_{\mathrm{LIAB}}$ component should explicitly include a
drought-contingent business-continuity sub-limit, with a parametric
trigger tied to municipal-water-restriction levels.

\subsection{Climate non-stationarity and the cooling reinsurance layer}\label{ss:cool-climate}

Equation~\eqref{eq:lambda-cool} contains $T_{\mathrm{wb}}(t)$ as a
deterministic-looking covariate, but it is in reality a non-stationary
stochastic process. We model it as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Twb-process}
   T_{\mathrm{wb}}(t) \;=\; \mu_{\mathrm{wb}}
   + \beta\, t
   + s(t)
   + \sigma_{\mathrm{wb}}\,B_t,
\end{equation}
with $\mu_{\mathrm{wb}}$ the long-run mean, $\beta>0$ a climate-drift
slope (empirically $\beta\approx 0.04$ K/yr for the major
hyperscale geographies under RCP 4.5), $s(t)$ a deterministic
seasonal kernel, and $B_t$ standard Brownian motion. The drift
$\beta$ shifts the cooling hazard upward by
$\dd\E[\lambda^{\mathrm{cool}}]/\dd t = \delta_T\,\beta\,
\E[\lambda^{\mathrm{cool}}]$, meaning that
\emph{a fully Tier-IV-rated site today drifts into Tier-III
effective performance over a decade if the cooling design is not
upgraded}. The reinsurance implication is direct: cooling-driven
losses are non-stationary, so a 10-year property treaty written at
today's $\lambda^{\mathrm{cool}}_0$ is under-priced unless the
trajectory is loaded in.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Operational risk: human factors, water treatment, controls}\label{ss:cool-ops}

Beyond the deterministic physics, cooling carries a substantial
operational-risk burden. The Uptime Institute's annual outage
analysis attributes a non-trivial fraction of cooling failures to:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
   \item \textbf{Water treatment.} Improper biocide dosing leads to
         \emph{Legionella} colonisation in cooling towers (a public
         health liability), or to galvanic corrosion of chiller
         heat exchangers (a property damage exposure).
   \item \textbf{Setpoint mismanagement.} The cold-aisle temperature
         setpoint is a finely-tuned trade-off: too low and PUE
         degrades; too high and equipment ages faster. Errors in
         setpoint procedures produce a tail of correlated
         multi-rack thermal events.
   \item \textbf{Building Management System (BMS) integrity.} The BMS
         is the soft target for cyber attacks aimed at physical
         disruption. An attacker who can mis-set chiller
         enable/disable signals can produce an outage with no
         visible electrical fault --- placing the loss in the
         $L^{(i)}_{\mathrm{CY}}$ component of~\eqref{eq:loss-decomp}
         even though the physical mechanism is purely thermal.
\end{itemize}

These mechanisms motivate a \emph{cooling-specific cyber-physical
coupling} that enters the dependence structure of
\S\ref{sec:dependence} with non-negligible Gumbel-copula tail
dependence $\lambda_U^{\mathrm{cool,cy}}\in[0.10,0.25]$.

\subsection{Implications for pricing, capital, and product design}\label{ss:cool-pricing}

We can now state the cooling-specific modifications to the
pricing framework of~\S\ref{sec:pricing}--\S\ref{sec:capital}:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
   \item \textbf{Frequency.} The Poisson--Gamma frailty intensity
         $\lambda_i$ of~\eqref{eq:GLM} acquires a cooling regressor:
         \begin{equation}\label{eq:GLM-cool}
            \log \lambda_i = \cdots
            + \beta_6\,\mathrm{Liquid}_i
            + \beta_7\,\mathrm{Climate}_i
            + \beta_8\,(\mathrm{PUE}_i - 1.10)^+,
         \end{equation}
         where $\mathrm{Liquid}_i\in\{0,1\}$ flags liquid/immersion
         architecture, $\mathrm{Climate}_i$ is a wet-bulb-derived
         hazard score, and the $(\mathrm{PUE}_i-1.10)^+$ term
         penalises sites operating below best-in-class efficiency.

   \item \textbf{Severity.} The GPD tail shape for cooling-driven
         losses is heavier:
         $\xi_{\mathrm{cool}}\in[0.3,0.7]$ versus
         $\xi_{\mathrm{elec}}\in[0.2,0.6]$.
         Sites with liquid or immersion cooling should be priced
         with a cooling-component tail loading $\theta_2$ scaled by
         $1+\eta_{\mathrm{cool}}$, with $\eta_{\mathrm{cool}}\approx 0.25$
         from broker-quoted comparisons.

   \item \textbf{Capital.} Solvency~II SCR contribution from cooling
         scales with $\TVaR_{0.995}(S^{\mathrm{cool}})$, which is
         disproportionately large because of (i) the heavier tail
         and (ii) the cooling--electrical correlation: a chilled-water
         leak on a power train brings both subsystems down at once.

   \item \textbf{Product.} The hybrid indemnity--parametric product
         of \S\ref{sec:product} acquires a third parametric trigger:
         a SCADA-measured PUE excursion or a sustained $\Psi>0.95$
         reading for more than a threshold duration. This is the
         cooling analogue of the voltage-sag trigger and shares the
         same basis-risk decomposition~\eqref{eq:basis2}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What an underwriter should ask}\label{ss:cool-underwriting}

In the same spirit as an arc-flash survey (\S\ref{ss:arcflash})
gives a direct estimator of the electrical-side $\xi$, a
\emph{cooling-system audit} produces the analogous estimator for
$\xi_{\mathrm{cool}}$. The audit data the underwriter should
require, in priority order, are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=4pt]
   \item Architecture flag: air / D2C liquid / immersion (and which
         fluid family from Table~\ref{tab:fluids}).
   \item Design wet-bulb temperature $T^*_{\mathrm{wb}}$ and the past
         five years of distribution-system wet-bulb measurements.
   \item PUE distribution (1-second resolution, full year) and its
         empirical CDF.
   \item Chiller plant single-line: redundancy ($N$, $N+1$, $2N$),
         compressor count, free-cooling capability.
   \item Coolant inventory and replacement-chain status (especially
         PFAS exposure under~\S\ref{ss:cool-chem}).
   \item Water source, water rights, drought-restriction history.
   \item BMS network architecture and the segmentation between BMS
         and IT-OT networks (the cyber-physical coupling lever).
   \item History of incident reports including
         pump-failure-near-misses, valve actuator failures, and
         setpoint-procedure deviations.
\end{enumerate}

A standardised cooling-audit dataset would do for cooling what the
NFPA 70E arc-flash survey does for electrical: convert a tacit
engineering-quality assessment into a one-number actuarial input.
\emph{The framework of this paper supports the integration of such an
audit directly through equation~\eqref{eq:GLM-cool}}.

\subsection{Synthesis: cooling closes the third leg of the actuarial triangle}\label{ss:cool-synth}

The main text has already closed the loop between electrical
reliability engineering and actuarial pricing
(\S\ref{ss:closure}), and between actuarial pricing and economic
welfare (\S\ref{ss:loop}). This section closes the third leg: the
loop between \emph{thermodynamics, climate, and chemistry} on the
engineering side, and the actuarial state variables $(\lambda, \xi,
\theta, U)$ on the pricing side. Equation~\eqref{eq:lambda-cool} is
the cooling analogue of the electrical hazard~\eqref{eq:hazard};
equation~\eqref{eq:GLM-cool} the cooling extension of the frequency
regression; the GPD-tail observation $\xi_{\mathrm{cool}}>\xi_{\mathrm{elec}}$
is the severity claim; and the audit checklist of
\S\ref{ss:cool-underwriting} is the metrology closure.

Without this third leg, an actuarial framework for hyperscale data
centers is incomplete --- it would mis-price exactly the class of
risks that the AI training era and the climate transition are now
generating.
